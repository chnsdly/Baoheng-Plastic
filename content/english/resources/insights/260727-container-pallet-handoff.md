A plastic-pallet test report can be useful evidence, but it does not approve a loaded shipping container. It may describe the pallet model, a load condition, and a support condition. It does not show who planned the container layout, checked the actual loaded footprint, selected restraint, or accepted the load before the doors close.

The practical action is simple: hand the approved pallet data to the person responsible for packing and securing the cargo, then obtain a route-specific container-load approval. Keep those two approvals separate.

The [IMO/ILO/UNECE CTU Code](https://unece.org/transport/intermodal-transport/imoilounece-code-practice-packing-cargo-transport-units-ctu-code) is a non-mandatory global code of practice for packing cargo transport units. It addresses the intermodal chain and the people who pack, secure, receive, and unpack cargo. It is useful context for this handoff; it is not a substitute for the route's applicable law, carrier instructions, or site procedure.

## Do not turn a pallet rating into a container approval

Ask what the supplier document actually covers. A pallet rating may be relevant to a stated load, support arrangement, temperature, handling method, or test method. A container plan has additional questions:

- What are the measured length, width, height, and gross mass of the wrapped unit load?
- Which pallet orientation, gaps, blocks, or securing method are proposed in this specific CTU?
- Is the load distribution suitable for the CTU, the route, and the carrier's limits?
- Who checks the load before closing the doors, and who receives it at the other end?

These questions do not reduce the importance of the pallet. They prevent a good pallet choice from being treated as proof that the complete transport arrangement is safe.

## Create a handoff record before the packing plan is final

Use a short, factual handoff record. It should not be a generic certificate or a promise that every shipment will behave the same way.

| Give the packer | Keep with the container plan |
| --- | --- |
| Exact pallet model, footprint, deck and base type | CTU type, route, carrier limits, and loading drawing |
| Actual unit-load dimensions after wrapping or strapping | Orientation, spacing, blocking, bracing, or other restraint proposed |
| Gross mass and known concentration points | Mass distribution and any special handling restrictions |
| Supplier conditions and exclusions for the pallet data | Packer's inspection, approval, and escalation owner |
| Product, temperature, fork-entry, and handling facts that need confirmation | Photos or measurements from the actual trial where the route warrants one |

The supplier can normally explain the quoted pallet and its stated conditions. The packer or the party controlling the loading plan needs to decide whether the real unit load and securing arrangement are suitable for that container movement. The buyer should make the owner visible instead of leaving the decision between a quotation and a loading dock.

## Check the real unit load, not only the nominal pallet

For container work, a 1200 x 1000 mm pallet is not automatically a 1200 x 1000 mm loaded unit. Carton bulge, edge protection, stretch film, labels, straps, and uneven placement can change the usable width and contact points. Measure the normal load after it is prepared.

This is especially important when a shipment is tightly arranged. UNECE's CTU material explains that tight stowage can help use space and limit cargo movement, but it also depends on cargo that can tolerate mutual contact and on suitable securing measures. Do not force a tight pattern if it crushes cartons, transfers force into a weak part of the load, or makes the door-end condition unsafe to open.

The existing [plastic pallet size guide for container loading](/resources/insights/2625-plastic-pallet-size-container-loading-guide/) helps choose a footprint around cartons and destination handling. This handoff is the next decision: who confirms that the completed unit load can be packed and secured for the actual CTU route.

## Keep three responsibilities separate

Confusion usually starts when one party is asked to approve something outside its evidence.

**Pallet supplier.** Confirm the specific model, supplied technical data, and the stated operating conditions. Flag what needs a sample or product-specific test.

**Packer or logistics owner.** Plan the CTU loading and securing method. Confirm the actual loaded dimensions, mass, distribution, contact surfaces, route constraints, and inspection point.

**Buyer, customer, or receiving site.** Confirm the commercial and receiving conditions: accepted pallet size, unloading method, return ownership, customer rules, and any product-specific restrictions.

The same company may perform more than one role. The important point is that each decision still has an owner and evidence.

## Use the handoff when an export route changes

Repeat the review when a material condition changes. Examples include a different carton, a heavier SKU, a new wrapping method, another pallet model, a different container type, a new destination receiver, or a revised carrier instruction. A prior shipment can inform the next one, but it is not automatic approval for a changed load.

If the immediate problem is cartons sliding, leaning, or rubbing in transit, start with the [load-shift prevention guide](/resources/insights/2628-plastic-pallet-load-shift-prevention-transport/). It explains how pallet, unit-load, and transport controls interact. If samples are being approved for a wider purchase, use the [bulk-order load-test guide](/resources/insights/2616-plastic-pallet-load-test-before-bulk-order/) to document the pallet conditions separately from the CTU plan.

## A practical release question

Before a container is released, ask:

> Do we have evidence for the pallet under its stated conditions, and a separately owned approval for this loaded unit in this CTU and route?

If either answer is unclear, pause the volume release. Obtain the missing dimensions, loading plan, product information, or responsible review. The CTU Code is guidance, not a universal legal approval, and a pallet supplier cannot certify a customer's complete container-packing operation from a pallet specification alone.

For the actual shipment, confirm applicable law and carrier requirements with the responsible logistics or compliance owner. Validate the chosen pallet, unit load, and handling method with the actual goods, container, and equipment before volume deployment.
